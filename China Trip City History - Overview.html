How to Use This Guide

Four cities, four very different roles in Chinese history: Beijing is the imperial and modern capital, Xi'an is the ancient capital and Silk Road gateway, Shanghai is the treaty-port-turned-megacity, and Changzhou is a quieter Jiangnan canal town rarely covered in Western trip guides. Each section below gives a compact timeline, the historical thread that matters most for that city, and the sites you'll likely actually stand in front of.

Tip

The same handful of dynasty names (Qin, Han, Tang, Song, Yuan, Ming, Qing) keep reappearing across all four cities — use the timeline below as an anchor whenever a section mentions one and you want the "when was that, relative to everything else" context.

Beijing

Beijing's story is the story of who controls China — it has been the seat of power for three of the last four major dynasties, and for the Republic and People's Republic since. Unlike Xi'an, Beijing's importance is a relatively late development: it only became a truly national capital under a non-Han conquest dynasty, and every ruler since has largely kept it there.

Ancient Origins to Yuan Dynasty (before 1271)

The area has been settled for over 3,000 years, known in antiquity as Ji and later as a northern frontier city of shifting names (Yanjing, Zhongdu) under successive kingdoms and the Liao and Jin dynasties. It mattered mainly as a military and trade post guarding the approach from the northern steppe — not yet the center of the country.

Yuan, Ming & Qing: Becoming the Capital (1271–1912)

Kublai Khan made the city his Yuan dynasty capital in 1271, naming it Khanbaliq ("Great Khan's City") — Marco Polo's account of visiting it is one of the reasons it entered European awareness. The Ming dynasty's Yongle Emperor rebuilt the city on a new plan starting in 1406, constructing the Forbidden City (completed 1420) as the imperial palace and giving Beijing its name ("Northern Capital"). The Qing dynasty, founded by Manchu conquerors in 1644, kept Beijing as capital and largely preserved the Ming layout, adding the Summer Palace as an imperial retreat. This 500-year run makes Beijing's historic core almost entirely a Ming/Qing-era city.

Modern Era (1912–present)

Beijing lost its capital status briefly to Nanjing under the early Republic (1912–1949), then regained it the moment the People's Republic of China was declared from Tiananmen Gate on October 1, 1949. It was the epicenter of Cultural Revolution-era upheaval in the 1960s–70s, the site of the 1989 Tiananmen Square protests, and has since transformed into a global capital, symbolized by hosting the 2008 Summer Olympics and the 2022 Winter Olympics.

Tip

Beijing rewards thinking in three historical layers when you visit: Ming/Qing imperial (Forbidden City, Temple of Heaven, Summer Palace), early-PRC monumental (Tiananmen Square, Great Hall of the People), and 21st-century global city (Olympic Green, CBD skyline).

Xi'an

If Beijing is where China's modern power sits, Xi'an is where Chinese civilization itself was forged. As Chang'an, it was the capital of more dynasties than any other Chinese city and, at its Tang-dynasty peak, arguably the largest and most cosmopolitan city on Earth.

Zhou, Qin & Han: Birth of an Empire (11th c. BCE – 220 CE)

The Xi'an region was the heartland of the Zhou dynasty and then, in 221 BCE, the base from which Qin Shi Huang unified China's warring states into the first centralized Chinese empire — he ruled from nearby Xianyang and is buried outside modern Xi'an under a vast mausoleum guarded by the Terracotta Army, discovered by farmers in 1974. The Han dynasty that followed made the city (then called Chang'an) its capital and opened the Silk Road west from here around the 2nd century BCE, turning the city into the eastern anchor of a trade network reaching to Rome.

Tang Dynasty Golden Age (618–907 CE)

Chang'an under the Tang was the world's largest city, with population estimates over a million — a planned grid of walled wards housing Persian, Sogdian, Japanese, Korean, and Arab merchants and monks alongside a flourishing poetry and Buddhist culture. The Big Wild Goose Pagoda was built in 652 CE to house Buddhist scriptures brought back from India by the monk Xuanzang — the same journey later fictionalized as Journey to the West.

Tip

Xi'an's Muslim Quarter traces directly back to this era — it grew from the Silk Road merchant communities the Tang capital hosted, and is still centered on the Great Mosque, first built in 742 CE.

After the Tang: Decline and Rediscovery (907 CE–present)

Chang'an lost its capital status after the Tang collapsed in 907 CE and was never again the seat of a unified Chinese empire, gradually renamed Xi'an ("Western Peace") under the Ming dynasty, which also built the massive Xi'an City Wall still standing today — one of the best-preserved ancient city walls in China. The city faded to regional importance until the Terracotta Army's 1974 discovery restored it to the map as one of China's top historical destinations.

Shanghai

Shanghai's history inverts the other three cities: it was a minor fishing and market town for most of Chinese history and became globally significant only in the last 180 years — first by force, through 19th-century foreign intervention, and more recently by design, as the engine of China's economic reform.

Before 1842: A Regional Market Town

Shanghai existed as a walled county town from the Yuan dynasty (13th century) onward, valued for its position at the mouth of the Yangtze River delta, but it was a secondary town compared to the older, wealthier Jiangnan cities inland (including Changzhou) for most of imperial history.

Treaty Port Era (1842–1943)

China's defeat in the First Opium War forced the 1842 Treaty of Nanjing, which opened Shanghai as one of five "treaty ports" to foreign trade. Britain, France, and the United States each carved out concession areas with their own laws and administration, and by the 1920s–30s Shanghai had become the "Paris of the East" — Asia's leading financial and entertainment hub, with a skyline of grand banking houses along the riverfront known as the Bund, alongside extreme inequality, organized crime, and a large refugee and expatriate population. Foreign control ended only when Japan occupied the city during World War II.

Communist Era to Reform (1949–present)

After 1949 the new Communist government shut down Shanghai's foreign concessions and nightlife economy, and the city settled into a more subdued role as an industrial center for decades. That changed dramatically in 1990, when Beijing designated the then-largely agricultural east-bank district of Pudong a special economic zone — it's now home to the futuristic skyline (Oriental Pearl Tower, Shanghai Tower) that faces the historic Bund across the Huangpu River, making Shanghai China's largest city and financial capital.

Tip

Standing on the Bund and looking across the river is literally looking across Shanghai's two defining eras at once: 1920s-30s foreign concession architecture on your side, post-1990 reform-era skyline on the other.

Changzhou

Changzhou is the least internationally known of the four, but it's a genuinely old Jiangnan city with a continuous history stretching back roughly 2,500 years — its low profile abroad has more to do with 20th-century tourism patterns than with its actual historical weight.

Ancient Wu Culture Origins (c. 547 BCE onward)

The area was settled during the Spring and Autumn period as part of the ancient state of Wu, with a walled town founded in the region by 547 BCE — making Changzhou's urban history roughly contemporary with early Rome. It has carried several names over the centuries (Yanling, Jinling, Piling) as it passed between kingdoms and dynasties.

The Grand Canal & Jiangnan Prosperity (Sui–Qing, 581–1912 CE)

Changzhou's fortunes rose with the Grand Canal, the massive Sui-dynasty engineering project (begun 581 CE) linking the Yangtze and Yellow River basins — the Changzhou Ancient Canal section still runs through the city today and made it a key stop on the north-south grain and trade route. Under the Tang and Song, and especially during the Ming and Qing, Changzhou belonged to the broader Jiangnan region — the wealthy, canal-linked cluster of cities (with Suzhou and Hangzhou) that became imperial China's cultural and economic heartland, known for silk, printing, and a high concentration of scholar-officials who passed the imperial examinations.

Modern Era (1912–present)

Changzhou industrialized early relative to the wider region and grew into a manufacturing center through the 20th century. Today it's known regionally as a lighter-weight, more livable alternative to Shanghai/Suzhou tourism circuits, with a mix of preserved canal-town streets, its historic Tianning Temple and pagoda, and modern attractions like Dinosaur Park (built on a genuine local fossil-discovery history).

Tip

Changzhou is a good place to see the Grand Canal actually functioning as a working waterway and canal-town streetscape, without the crowds of the more famous canal-town stops like Suzhou or Wuzhen.

Why is Changzhou less famous than Suzhou or Hangzhou?

All three were part of the same historic Jiangnan canal-city cluster, but Suzhou and Hangzhou developed more extensive, better-preserved classical garden districts that became centerpieces of 20th-century tourism promotion (Suzhou's gardens are UNESCO-listed). Changzhou industrialized more heavily and earlier, which reshaped more of its historic core — it's not that its history is thinner, its surviving historic fabric is just smaller and less marketed internationally.
