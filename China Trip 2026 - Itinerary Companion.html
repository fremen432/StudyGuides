Shanghai 🌆 Oct 18–21

You're staying right on Nanjing Road, steps from the Bund — the two streets that, together, are the story of treaty-port Shanghai. See the overview guide for the full 1842-onward arc; here's what's specifically worth knowing at each stop on your list.

🛍️ Nanjing Road Pedestrian Street (your hotel's street)

Officially named in 1865 under the British-run Shanghai Municipal Council, Nanjing Road grew from a horse-racetrack access path into China's premier shopping street by the early 1900s, when department stores like Wing On and Sincere opened here — Chinese-owned businesses competing directly with foreign ones on a foreign-administered street, a small preview of the city's whole character. It was pedestrianized in 1999 and is now one of the busiest retail strips in the world; walking it at night, the neon is a direct descendant of its 1920s–30s reputation as the brightest street in Asia.

🥢 Ye Shanghai (夜上海) — dinner, Oct 18

The name literally means "Shanghai Nights" — it's a direct nod to the city's 1930s jazz-age nostalgia, the same golden era the Bund's architecture comes from. Restaurants and bars trading on that "Paris of the East" aesthetic (Art Deco interiors, qipao-clad staff, Shanghainese comfort food) are their own small tourism-and-memory industry in the city today.

🌃 The Bund, at night & by Huangpu River cruise

The riverfront row of banks and trading houses you'll see lit up were built mostly in the 1920s–30s by foreign banks and trading houses operating under extraterritorial law inside the International Settlement. Your cruise departs from Shiliupu Marina, one of Shanghai's oldest working docks — wharves here handled passenger and cargo traffic on the Huangpu since the Qing dynasty, long before the Bund's grand facades existed; you're leaving from the working harbor to go look at the monuments that harbor's wealth built.

Tip

Looking across the river from the Bund at night is looking at two different eras at once: the 1920s–30s foreign-concession skyline behind you, the post-1990 Pudong skyline (Oriental Pearl Tower, Shanghai Tower) in front — Pudong was mostly farmland until Beijing designated it a special economic zone in 1990.

🏛️ Wukang Road

Laid out in 1907 as "Route Ferguson" inside the French Concession, this is one of the best-preserved stretches of 1920s–30s garden-villa architecture in the city. The flatiron building at its main intersection — Wukang Mansion, built 1924 by Hungarian-Slovak architect László Hudec, shaped like a ship's bow — is one of Shanghai's most photographed pieces of Concession-era architecture. It's now a relaxed, cafe-lined street, a good contrast to the density of Nanjing Road and the Bund.

🛕 Jing'an Temple

One of the oldest institutions in the city by far — first founded in 247 CE during the Three Kingdoms period near Suzhou Creek, and relocated to its current site in 1216 during the Southern Song dynasty. Repeatedly rebuilt after fires and wars, its current gold-roofed halls (largely rebuilt in the 2000s) now sit directly across from a modern shopping mall and subway station — arguably Shanghai's starkest single "1,700 years of history vs. today" juxtaposition.

📍 Open in Google Maps

🌳 Yu Garden & Shanghai City God Temple

Yu Garden was built in 1559 by Ming-dynasty official Pan Yunduan as a gift for his father — a classical Jiangnan garden of the same style you'll see again, on a smaller scale, in Changzhou. Right next to it, the City God Temple was built in 1403 (Ming, Yongle era) to house the deities believed to protect the city; the surrounding bazaar that grew up around temple worship and pilgrim traffic is still the dense shopping/snack district it's been for 600 years — the commercial pattern predates the temple's religious purpose becoming secondary to tourists.

Changzhou 🚣 Oct 21–24

This is home ground — Xing Xing grew up here, and the Lao Zha apartment and old school are on the itinerary before the more typical sightseeing starts. Changzhou is the least internationally known of your four stops, but it's a genuinely old Jiangnan canal city; the overview guide covers its Sui-to-Qing arc, so here's the color behind the specific places you're actually walking through.

🏠 Lao Zha & Zhonglou District

The apartment and Xing Xing's old school sit in Zhonglou, one of Changzhou's central old districts — historically part of the walled city core, now a mix of older residential blocks and newer development. There's no separate "history" to look up here beyond what it already is for your family, but it's worth knowing you're inside the same old-city footprint that the Grand Canal and the historic lanes below run through — it's all one continuous urban fabric.

🐼 Hongmei Park (红梅公园)

Built around the Ming-dynasty Wenbi Pagoda ("Pen-Tip Pagoda"), constructed in 1611 — the tall, narrow shape was deliberately chosen under feng shui belief that a pen-shaped tower would boost the local district's luck in the imperial civil service examinations, the path to government office. The park itself was assembled in 1958 around the historic pagoda grounds and has since grown into one of Changzhou's main public parks, panda 🐼 exhibit included.

📍 Open in Google Maps

🛕 Tianning Temple (天宁寺)

Founded in 646 CE under Tang Emperor Taizong, making it more than 1,300 years older than almost everything else on this trip except the Xi'an sites — it's one of the "Four Great Monasteries" of Chinese Chan (Zen) Buddhism, alongside temples in Zhenjiang, Yangzhou, and Ningbo. It's been rebuilt repeatedly after fire and war, most notably after Taiping Rebellion damage in the 1860s. The modern 153.79-meter Tianning Pagoda on the grounds, completed in 2007, was briefly the tallest pagoda in the world — a genuinely ancient monastery with a genuinely new record-holding tower standing right next to each other.

📍 Open in Google Maps

🏮 Qingguo Lane (青果巷) — the "downtown" lane on your list

This is arguably the single most historically interesting stop of your Changzhou days and easy to walk past without realizing it. Construction here predates 1581 (Ming, Wanli era); the name means roughly "Green Fruit Lane," from its original role as a produce-trading street along the canal. Over the Qing dynasty it became a residential enclave for Changzhou's scholar and merchant elite, and it's the birthplace or family home of a striking number of major modern Chinese figures — including Qu Qiubai (an early leader of the Chinese Communist Party) and linguist Zhao Yuanren (Yuen Ren Chao), among others. The lane was restored as a historic district preserving its canal-house architecture, and is now the shops/cafes stretch on your itinerary — worth a slower walk than a typical "downtown lunch stop."

📍 Open in Google Maps

🎋 Nanshan Bamboo Sea (南山竹海)

Less about a specific historical event and more about a cultural thread: bamboo has been a loaded symbol in Chinese literati culture for over a thousand years, prized by Tang and Song poets and painters 🖌️ (Song-dynasty polymath Su Dongpo's famous line "I'd rather eat without meat than live without bamboo" is the classic reference point) as a symbol of resilience and moral integrity — bending in the wind without breaking. The pandas are a modern tourism addition (they aren't native to this part of Jiangsu), but the reverence for bamboo forests itself is old.

Xi'an 🏺 Oct 24–26

Two dense days built almost entirely around the Tang dynasty's golden age and the Qin dynasty's founding of empire — the overview guide covers Xi'an's full Zhou-through-Ming arc; here's the story behind each specific stop.

🕌 Muslim Quarter / Dasi (大寺) Residential Quarter — dinner, Oct 24

This neighborhood grew directly out of the Tang-dynasty Silk Road trade community, centered on the Great Mosque (first built 742 CE, expanded through the Ming), which blends Chinese temple-style architecture with Islamic function — no domes or minarets, just a mosque built to look like a Chinese courtyard complex. The Hui Muslim community here has been continuous for over a thousand years; the street food you'll eat (biang biang noodles, roujiamo, yangrou paomo) is that community's cuisine, not a tourist invention.

📍 Open in Google Maps

🏮 Datang Everbright City (大唐不夜城) — after dinner, Oct 24

A modern (largely 2011–2019) pedestrian and light-show district built right next to the Big Wild Goose Pagoda, deliberately designed with Tang-dynasty architectural styling and a row of statues depicting Tang-era poets and scholars. It's essentially the city packaging its own golden age into a night-life district — a useful, very literal illustration of just how central the Tang is to Xi'an's civic identity today, nearly 1,100 years after the dynasty ended.

📍 Open in Google Maps

🗿 Terracotta Warriors — Oct 25 morning

Built starting around 246 BCE to guard the tomb of Qin Shi Huang, China's first unifying emperor, and discovered entirely by accident in March 1974 by farmers digging a well. Pit 1, the largest and the one you'll spend the most time in, holds an entire buried infantry army in battle formation — each figure was individually sculpted with a distinct face, and all were originally painted in vivid color that faded within minutes of exposure to air after 2,000+ years underground, which is why archaeologists now leave large sections of newly found pits unexcavated until better preservation techniques exist. UNESCO-listed since 1987.

📍 Open in Google Maps

♨️ Huaqing Palace — Oct 25 afternoon

Hot springs at this site were used by royalty as far back as the Zhou dynasty, but its fame comes from Tang Emperor Xuanzong, who had it rebuilt into a grand palace in 723 CE as a retreat for himself and his celebrated consort, Yang Guifei — one of the "Four Beauties" of Chinese history. Their romance, and Yang Guifei's death during the An Lushan Rebellion that followed, was immortalized in Bai Juyi's Tang-dynasty poem "Song of Everlasting Sorrow" (长恨歌) — which is exactly the show you're seeing that evening, performed outdoors against the mountain backdrop where the story is actually set. 🎭

Tip

Huaqing Palace has a second, much more recent claim to fame: it's where the 1936 Xi'an Incident happened — Nationalist leader Chiang Kai-shek was detained here by his own generals to force a truce with the Communists and a united front against Japan. Worth mentioning if the tour guide only tells the Tang-dynasty love story.

Beijing 🏯 Oct 26–31

The imperial and modern capital, and the itinerary's longest stop. The overview guide covers the Yuan-Ming-Qing-to-present arc in full; here's what to know about each specific site and day.

🏯 Tian'anmen Square & The Forbidden City — Oct 27

The square in front of the Forbidden City's main gate was expanded to its current million-person scale in the 1950s; it's where the People's Republic of China was declared on October 1, 1949. Walk straight through it into the Forbidden City itself, built 1406–1420 under the Ming Yongle Emperor and home to 24 emperors across the Ming and Qing dynasties — you're covering roughly 500 years of continuous imperial rule in a single day's walk from square to palace.

📍 Tiananmen Square on Google Maps · Forbidden City on Google Maps

⛩️ Temple of Heaven — Oct 27

Built the same years as the Forbidden City (completed 1420) but for a different purpose: this is where Ming and Qing emperors performed annual rites asking Heaven for good harvests. The circular Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests, entirely wooden and built without a single nail, is the single most photographed piece of non-palace imperial architecture in the city.

📍 Open in Google Maps

🧱 The Great Wall — Oct 28

Not one wall but a patchwork built and rebuilt across many dynasties over roughly 2,000 years; the sections closest to Beijing, and the ones typically visited, are Ming-dynasty reconstructions built specifically to defend the capital from northern incursions after the capital moved here in the 15th century — so the wall you'll walk is, in a real sense, the same Ming building project as the Forbidden City and Temple of Heaven, just aimed outward instead of inward.

📍 Open in Google Maps

🏛️ Old Summer Palace (Yuanmingyuan) & Summer Palace (Yihe Yuan) — Oct 29

These two sites, visited back to back, are a deliberate before-and-after. The Old Summer Palace was an 18th-century Qing "garden of gardens," including European-style palace buildings partly designed with input from Jesuit missionaries at the Qing court — then burned and looted by British and French forces in 1860 during the Second Opium War, in retaliation during treaty negotiations. The ruins were deliberately never fully repaired, left standing as a national memorial to that humiliation. The Summer Palace next door is what replaced it in the public imagination: rebuilt and expanded in the late 1800s by Empress Dowager Cixi (controversially using funds diverted from the imperial navy's budget), centered on Kunming Lake and Longevity Hill, with the famous Marble Boat pavilion. One site shows what foreign intervention destroyed; the other shows how the court chose to spend its way past that loss.

📍 Old Summer Palace on Google Maps · Summer Palace on Google Maps

🏮 Hutong & Houhai — evenings

Beijing's hutong (narrow lane) and siheyuan (courtyard house) pattern dates to the original Yuan-dynasty grid plan of the 13th century — the alleys you'll walk are following a street layout roughly 750 years old, even though most individual buildings are newer. Houhai, the lake district behind it, was originally part of the Yuan-dynasty Grand Canal terminus, dug to bring grain and goods into the capital by water; it later became a recreational area for imperial-era elite (Prince Gong's Mansion sits on its shore), and today's bar street is simply the latest use of the same lakeside real estate.

Tip

便宜坊 (Pianyifang), the roast duck restaurant near the Ciyunsi area, claims a founding date of 1416 — older than the more internationally famous Quanjude (founded 1864). It uses "menlu" (closed-oven) roasting rather than Quanjude's open hung-oven style, so the duck itself is a genuinely different, older tradition, not just a competing brand.
